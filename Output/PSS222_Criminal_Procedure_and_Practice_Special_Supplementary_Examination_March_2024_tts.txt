Let's begin with Question One, which concerns police powers and preliminary investigations.
Part A asks how crime suspects are identified and interrogated. Begin your answer by identifying the recognized identification methods: eyewitness identification parades, photographic line-ups, DNA profiling, and fingerprint analysis. For instance, in an identification parade, the police must ensure absolute procedural fairness by placing the suspect among eight or more persons of similar age, height, and general appearance. When discussing interrogation, emphasize constitutional thresholds under Article 49 of the Constitution of Kenya: the right to remain silent, prompt access to an advocate, and the prohibition against torture, coercion, or cruel treatment to extract confessions.
Part B asks for the procedure of arrest under Section 21 of the Criminal Procedure Code. Explain the three statutory steps: first, the arresting officer must touch or confine the body of the person to be arrested unless there is submission by word or action; second, the officer must inform the suspect of the true reason for the arrest promptly in a language they understand; third, the officer must use only reasonable and necessary force if the suspect forcibly resists or attempts to evade arrest. Mention that excessive force renders the arrest procedurally flawed.

Question Two deals with search warrants and challenging unlawful police custody.
Part A requires defining a search warrant and explaining four essential contents. A search warrant is a formal judicial order issued by a magistrate or judge empowering law enforcement officers to enter a specified premises to search for and seize designated evidence, property, or individuals. Four mandatory contents include: the specific description of the premises to be searched, the exact category of articles or items sought, the statutory offence under investigation, and the signature, seal, and expiry date issued by the competent judicial officer.
Part B asks under what circumstances one can challenge an unlawful arrest. An arrest can be challenged through a constitutional petition under Article 22 for fundamental rights violations, through habeas corpus proceedings to compel production of a detainee held beyond twenty-four hours, by filing civil claims for false imprisonment and malicious prosecution, or by raising preliminary procedural objections during arraignment.

Question Three explores systemic decision-making points and post-conviction relief.
In Part A, map out the criminal justice funnel: law enforcement investigation, the arrest decision, the prosecutorial decision to charge based on public interest and evidentiary sufficiency, initial arraignment and plea taking, pre-trial bail or bond determination, the trial hearing, adjudication of guilt, sentencing, and appellate reviews. Explain how discretion is exercised at each gate.
In Part B, examine post-conviction relief. Discuss circumstances such as the discovery of fresh, compelling exculpatory evidence that was not available at trial, proof of grave procedural irregularities like denial of legal representation, retroactive application of new sentencing jurisprudence, or executive clemency and pardon via the Power of Mercy under Article 133 of the Constitution.

Question Four examines procedural due process and trial participants.
Part A asks for five elements of procedural due process: notice of charges detailing the accusations, an impartial and independent judicial tribunal, the opportunity to be heard and present witnesses, the right to cross-examine prosecution witnesses, and a reasoned judgment based strictly on admissible evidence.
Part B explores the distinct duties of prosecution and defense counsel. The prosecution represents the public interest; their primary duty is not merely to secure a conviction at all costs, but to ensure justice is served by presenting all material evidence, including disclosing exculpatory material to the defense. The defense lawyer, conversely, serves as a zealous advocate for the accused, ensuring constitutional safeguards are upheld, challenging state evidence, and safeguarding the presumption of innocence.

Question Five invites you to analyze four theoretical models of justice.
Contrast Herbert Packer's Crime Control Model, which prioritizes speed, efficiency, and repression of criminal conduct like an assembly line, with the Due Process Model, which operates like an obstacle course prioritizing individual rights and judicial integrity. Then explain the Justice Model, emphasizing proportionality and determinate sentencing; the Rehabilitation Model, viewing criminal behavior as a treatable pathology; and the Restorative Justice Perspective, which shifts focus from punishing the offender to repairing harm among the victim, offender, and community.

Question Six asks for the chronological steps of a criminal trial.
Structure your answer into clear, logical phases: first, plea taking and reading of the charge; second, the prosecution opening statement where applicable; third, examination-in-chief, cross-examination, and re-examination of prosecution witnesses; fourth, closing of the prosecution case and the judicial ruling on whether a prima facie case has been established under Section 211 of the Criminal Procedure Code. If a prima facie case is established, the accused is put on their defense, where they may choose to give sworn evidence, an unsworn statement, or remain silent. Finally, both sides deliver closing submissions, followed by judgment, mitigation, and sentencing.

Question Seven focuses on constitutional trial rights and penology.
Part A requires five trial rights under Article 50 of the Constitution: the presumption of innocence until proven guilty, the right to be informed of the charge with sufficient detail, adequate time and facilities to prepare a defense, the right to choose and be represented by an advocate, and the right not to be compelled to give self-incriminating evidence.
Part B asks for four penological justifications: deterrence (discouraging the offender and the public from committing crimes), retribution (proportional punishment expressing societal condemnation), incapacitation (protecting society by removing dangerous offenders), and rehabilitation (reforming offenders through education and counseling).

Question Eight addresses community-based non-custodial sentencing versus incarceration.
Discuss the tangible advantages of non-custodial sentences, such as Community Service Orders and probation: they alleviate severe prison overcrowding, reduce state expenditure on prison maintenance, prevent first-time offenders from being hardened by career criminals in prison, preserve family cohesion and economic livelihoods, and foster genuine restorative accountability through community service.
